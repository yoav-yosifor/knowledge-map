## Core Idea

A hidden-variable theory assumes that quantum mechanics is incomplete, and that additional, unobserved parameters $\lambda$ actually determine measurement outcomes. In the local version of this idea, a local hidden-variable (LHV) model, each outcome is a function only of the local measurement setting and $\lambda$. It never depends on a distant party's measurement choice.

## Derivation Outline

1. Assume quantum mechanics is incomplete: outcomes are actually fixed by extra hidden parameters $\lambda$, not genuinely random.
2. Restrict to local hidden-variable models: an outcome depends only on the local setting and $\lambda$, never on the distant party's choice.
3. This locality restriction gives the model a specific, falsifiable structure for how distant correlations must be built up.
4. Test that structural claim against experiment via the CHSH Inequality.

## Physical Intuition

The idea behind hidden variables is a very old and very reasonable scientific instinct: if a theory only predicts probabilities, maybe that's because we're missing some piece of the story, some extra property of the particle that we simply haven't learned to measure yet, and if we knew it we'd be able to predict every outcome with certainty. A local hidden-variable model tries to build exactly that missing piece, but insists on one very natural restriction — whatever this extra hidden property is, it has to belong to each particle individually, and what happens to one particle's outcome can only depend on that particle's own hidden property and on which measurement was chosen for it, never on some faraway choice being made by an experimenter at the same instant on the other particle. That restriction is what makes the idea testable at all: it's a specific, falsifiable claim about how correlations between distant particles have to be built up, one local piece at a time, rather than through some instantaneous link between the two measurement stations. The whole point of hidden-variable theories is that they're meant to reproduce everything quantum mechanics predicts while still keeping this common-sense, no-spooky-action structure intact — and that's precisely the claim that gets put to the test experimentally.
