## Core Idea

This is [EPR](EPR%20Argument.md)'s proposed criterion for an "element of reality." If the value of a physical quantity can be predicted with certainty without disturbing the system, then that quantity must correspond to a pre-existing element of physical reality. In other words, measurement outcomes are assumed to reveal pre-existing values, not values created by the act of measurement itself.

## Derivation Outline

1. State the criterion: if a quantity's value can be predicted with certainty without disturbing the system, it is a genuine element of reality.
2. Note that entangled particles allow exactly this: measuring one predicts the outcome on the distant partner with certainty.
3. That prediction is made without ever touching or disturbing the distant particle.
4. Conclude (per EPR) that the predicted quantity must have been a pre-existing property, fixed before and independent of the measurement.

## Physical Intuition

This is really a proposed criterion for when it's fair to say something was "really there" before anyone measured it, and it's meant to sound almost too obviously true to argue with. If you can, without ever touching or disturbing a system in any way, predict with total certainty what some measurement on it would give, then the claim is that this predicted value must have already existed as a genuine property of the system, independent of the act of measurement — otherwise, where would the certainty be coming from? The intuition is drawn straight from ordinary experience: if you know for certain a coin in a sealed box landed heads, without opening the box, that's because it already landed heads before you predicted it, not because your prediction somehow made it land that way. The idea is to apply that same everyday logic to quantum mechanics, using perfect correlations between distant, entangled particles as the tool for making certain predictions without disturbance. The entire weight of the later argument built on this idea rests on this one criterion being reasonable: if you accept it, then predicting an outcome with certainty forces you to admit that outcome was fixed all along, in stark tension with the standard quantum-mechanical claim that a measurement outcome is genuinely undetermined until the measurement actually happens.
