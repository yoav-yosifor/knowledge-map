## Core Idea

Given two identical copies of an unknown quantum state, no physical process can delete one copy while leaving the other completely unchanged. This is the time-reverse counterpart of the [No-Cloning Theorem](No-Cloning%20Theorem.md). Together, the two theorems express a conservation of quantum information under unitary evolution.

## Derivation Outline

1. Assume two copies of an unknown $|\psi\rangle$ exist and suppose some unitary could delete one, leaving a blank state there.
2. Closed-system dynamics must be unitary, hence perfectly reversible.
3. True deletion would erase $|\psi\rangle$'s information with no trace transferred elsewhere.
4. That loss cannot be undone by running the process backward — contradicts reversibility.
5. So no unitary can perfectly delete an unknown state — the time-reverse of no-cloning.

## Physical Intuition

If no-cloning says you can't manufacture a second copy of an unknown quantum state out of nothing, no-deleting is the mirror-image statement: given two copies that already exist, you can't make one of them vanish back into a blank slate while leaving the other copy completely untouched. The reason both of these are true for the same underlying reason is that quantum evolution, whenever nothing is being measured and no information is leaking out to an environment, is unitary — which means it's perfectly reversible, like running a movie backwards and getting exactly the frame you started with. Genuinely erasing one of the two copies would mean the information that used to be sitting in that copy has simply ceased to exist, with no trace of it left anywhere in the universe to reconstruct where it went — and that's exactly the kind of information loss that reversible, unitary dynamics forbids by construction, because a reversible process must be able to be run backwards, and you can't run "erasing information" backwards if the information genuinely isn't anywhere anymore. So no-cloning and no-deleting are really the same physical fact — the total amount of quantum information described by a closed system is conserved — looked at from two different directions: you can't create it from nothing, and you can't destroy it into nothing.
