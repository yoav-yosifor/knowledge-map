## Core Idea

Local realism combines two assumptions. Realism says that measurement outcomes are determined by pre-existing properties. [Locality](Locality.md) says that those properties are independent of distant measurement choices. A theory satisfying both is called locally realistic, and the [CHSH Inequality](CHSH%20Inequality.md) shows that quantum mechanics is not one.

## Derivation Outline

1. Take the realism assumption: outcomes are determined by pre-existing properties, revealed (not created) by measurement.
2. Take the locality assumption: those properties can't depend on a distant, simultaneous measurement choice.
3. Combine the two: each particle carries a predetermined outcome for every possible measurement, fixed independently of distant settings.
4. This conjunction yields a testable constraint on correlations between distant particles — the CHSH Inequality.
5. Quantum mechanics violates that constraint, showing no locally realistic theory can reproduce all quantum predictions.

## Physical Intuition

Local realism is really two separate, individually very reasonable-sounding assumptions bolted together. The realism half says that a particle's properties exist and have definite values before anyone measures them — the measurement just reveals a pre-existing fact rather than creating the outcome on the spot. The locality half says that whatever those pre-existing values are for one particle, they can't depend on some choice being made by an experimenter far away, working on a different, distant particle, at the same moment. Put those two together and you get a theory where each particle privately carries around its own complete answer sheet, filled out in advance, for every measurement anyone might ever choose to perform on it, and that answer sheet was written down independently of anything happening anywhere else at the same time. This combined picture feels like nothing more than ordinary common sense about how an objective, causal universe ought to behave — which is exactly why it was such a striking result when a specific statistical test built on this combined assumption turned out to predict a strict numerical limit on certain correlations between distant particles, a limit that quantum mechanics itself blows straight through. Nature, it turns out, refuses to be described by any theory built from both of these assumptions at once, even though each one, taken alone, still sounds perfectly reasonable.
